In March I wrote about violence described in the language of precision and strategy. On April 10, [a man was arrested after a Molotov cocktail was thrown at Sam Altman's home around four in the morning](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/apr/10/sam-altman-home-molotov-cocktail).

The suspect was reported to be twenty. I understand why fears about AI can feel urgent. They don't justify an attack on a person. It makes someone a target and leaves the question of safety unanswered.

Afterward, [Altman posted a family photo and wrote about Tolkien's Ring of Power](https://blog.samaltman.com/2279512). By the Ring, he means the desire to control AGI alone, not AGI itself: *"The only solution I can come up with is to orient towards sharing the technology with people broadly, and for no one to have the ring."* I keep thinking about Boromir. He thought he could put the Ring to good use too.

The scale of OpenAI's fundraising sits awkwardly beside that sentence. In the same week, [The New Yorker published an investigation by Ronan Farrow and Andrew Marantz](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/04/13/sam-altman-may-control-our-future-can-he-be-trusted), based on more than a hundred interviews. One former board member called Altman "unconstrained by truth"; Altman disputes the allegations. OpenAI also [proposed taxes on automated labor, a public wealth fund and trials of four-day workweeks](https://openai.com/index/industrial-policy-for-the-intelligence-age/).

I can read those proposals on their merits without taking them as a substitute for independent scrutiny. The investigation raises questions about whom to trust with decisions about AI. My old question about whose adolescence this is feels too distant now. I want to know who gets to check the people building these systems.
